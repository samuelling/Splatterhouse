# Splatterhaus

A real-time 3D Gaussian splat demo in one HTML file: birds at dusk over a wildflower meadow with a barn, a silo, fenced fields and a mix of trees, running out through marram dunes to a beach and the sea, where the sun is setting. Seven kinds of flier are on the menu, each with its own rules: starlings in a murmuration, dunlin, jackdaws, pigeons, geese in V formation, white storks circling in thermals, and a speculative reconstruction of *Pteranodon*. Flocks split apart and rejoin, and up to four peregrine falcons can attack at once.

Every splat is generated in the browser and drawn the way trained splat scenes are. Each one is an anisotropic 3D Gaussian (position, rotation, scale, colour, opacity), projected to a 2D covariance in the vertex shader, depth-sorted on the CPU each frame and alpha-blended back to front. The one exception is the shotgun of Splatterhaus mode, which is a triangle mesh.

## Run it

Open `index.html`, or serve the repo with GitHub Pages. It needs WebGL2 and has no build step. Everything is in the one file except the optional finger-gun aiming, which fetches a hand tracker from the network when you switch it on.

## Splatterhaus mode

Press `B`, or the button under the title. You are on foot in the barn field with a pump-action shotgun, and that field is the only place you can shoot from: the roadside fence, the two side fences and the edge of the dunes where they stop hold you in, and the barn and the trees are solid. Choosing any other view puts the gun away.

| **Aim** | The cross-hairs follow the mouse. The arrow keys turn your head and bring the cross-hairs back to the middle; dragging turns your head too. The ring shows how wide the pattern is. Scroll or pinch to zoom. |
| **Walk** | `W` `A` `S` `D`, `Shift` to run. There is no walking on a touch screen. |

- **The shot.** 34 pellets, scattered about 1.2° either side of the aim (the ring is drawn at about twice that). Each pellet stops in the first bird it meets, so in a dense murmuration one shot can take a dozen or more and in a skein of geese one or two. Every pellet counts out to 75 m, fewer and fewer beyond, none past 170 m, which is far more generous than a real shotgun. Dunlin and *Pteranodon* keep to the shore, so walk to the seaward end of the field for those. A falcon inside the pattern dies too.
- **The birds.** A hit bird is taken out of the flock model that instant and becomes a falling body: the same splats, bloodied, tumbling, trailing drops, landing in the grass or splashing into the sea. It lies where it fell for a while. Around each hit there is a burst of blood, a red mist and a puff of feathers, all splats; some drops leave stains where they land. A kill within about 25 m spatters the lens.
- **The flock.** The rest flee the shot. For starlings, dunlin, jackdaws and pigeons it works like a falcon appearing in their midst: those near it bolt and the alarm passes from neighbour to neighbour as a wave. Geese near the shot break formation and re-form; soarers flinch away. The count, the slider and the flock statistics all follow the losses. When three birds are left, a fresh flock arrives.
- **The dog.** A black-and-white farm collie in a red neckerchief, drawn as 8-bit pixel art and about a metre tall, comes out from the barn and waits near you. After a shot that brings birds down on land it runs out, picks up one of them (the nearest; one bird from each shot, however many fell), carries it back crosswise in its mouth and lays it in front of you, then sits. There is a square-wave bark at each end. You see the dog side on when it crosses your view, from behind when it runs away from you and from the front when it comes back. Birds that fall in the sea are lost. It runs in bounds, as dogs do in long grass, goes round the barn, and is not kept in by the fence. Each pixel of the sprite is one splat drawn as a hard square facing the eye, sorted in with all the others; the dog and the bird in its mouth are drawn as if a little nearer than they are, or the grass would bury them.
- **The bed.** Where the dog lays the birds, the grass is pressed flat: the meadow shader lays the blades down and splays them outward in a patch about a metre across and takes the flowers out of it, so the birds lie in a nest of flattened grass at their true depth, with the standing grass around them in front where it should be. The patch stays where the first bird was laid while you stay within five metres of it, and springs back when the birds have gone.
- **The gun.** Not splats. It is a triangle mesh loaded at start-up from a real glTF model embedded in the page, lit by the same low sun and sky as everything else, and drawn after the splats with its own depth buffer, because a gun in your hands is always the nearest thing in view. It follows the cross-hairs, lags a little behind a turn of the head, kicks on firing, and is pumped after every shot with the fore-end sliding back and a spent case thrown clear. To reload it is canted over and cartridges are pushed one at a time into the port under the receiver; if it was empty it is racked at the end. The cartridges and the muzzle flash are small meshes made in code. There are no hands.
- **The sound.** The shots, the pump and the dry click are recordings. Each shot adds the top of the crack, which the vintage recording has lost, a low thump, three echoes off the barn and the trees, and a roll of reverberation. The pump animation is timed to whichever of four pump recordings is playing. A cartridge going home is the first click of a pump recording with a soft thump under it, since I found no recording of that under a clear licence. Hits are synthesised and arrive late by the speed of sound.

- **Finger gun.** Switch it on and the page asks for the camera. The picture is read in the browser by MediaPipe's hand tracker, which returns 21 points on the hand; no picture leaves the computer.
  - *The gesture.* The index finger out, with or without the middle finger beside it as a two-finger barrel, and the ring and little fingers folded. It is judged by how much further out the index finger is than those two, so an open hand and a fist are both rejected whatever the angle. It takes two readings in a row to become a gun and six to stop being one, and a hand lost for a moment keeps its aim, so it should not flicker.
  - *The other hand.* It is a joystick. Where it first appears is the stick's centre (a dashed ring in the camera picture). Held open, moving it away from the centre turns your head that way; as a fist, moving it up walks you forward, down back, and sideways sidesteps. Further from the centre is faster, with a dead patch in the middle, and changing between open hand and fist re-centres the stick. The two hands are told apart by three kinds of evidence: a clear finger gun; where each hand was a moment ago; and the tracker's own label of left or right, which is learnt for the gun hand while it shoots (by majority, because the label wavers when a hand points straight at the camera). Position settles it while the hands are apart, and the label settles it when they cross or one has been out of view. Aiming with the other hand for a moment hands the gun over.
  - *Aiming.* The fingertip (or the point between two fingertips), mirrored, steers the cross-hairs. This is where the hand is in the camera's view, not the true direction the finger points.
  - *Firing.* Drop the thumb, like a hammer falling. The shot goes where the gun pointed a moment before, because the thumb jogs the hand.
  - *Calibration.* Runs the first time, and again from the Calibrate button under the camera picture; `Esc` skips it. Four steps: point at a dot in the middle with the thumb up, drop the thumb, point at a dot near the top-left corner, then one near the bottom-right. Each step waits until your hand has reached its place and is holding still, then reads it for a second while the dot fills, starting again if the hand moves, and pauses on "Got it" before the next. It learns how strongly your finger gun reads, how far your thumb travels, and how far your hand moves to cover the screen, and keeps them in the browser for next time.
  - *Steadiness.* The aim goes through a one-euro filter: heavy smoothing while the hand is nearly still, so the cross-hairs do not tremble, and little while it moves fast, so they do not lag.
  - *The picture in the corner.* What the camera sees, with a dot on the fingertip: grey when a hand is seen but not as a gun, red when it is aiming, yellow as it fires. The other hand shows as a blue dot when it is turning your head and a green square when it is walking you. The two numbers under it are the finger score and the thumb reading, which are what to quote if it misbehaves.
  - *What it needs.* The tracker (`@mediapipe/tasks-vision` 1.2.3, Apache-2.0) and its model are not in this file: about 22 MB is fetched from jsDelivr and Google's model store the first time, and about 4 MB more for the face model if head look is used. So it needs a network, and a page that is allowed to use the camera, which a page shown inside another site usually is not.
  - *How far it has been tested.* By me: with the real tracker on a dummy camera, and with made-up hand shapes. The thresholds were first guesses; the calibration exists because the first real hand to try it found them too strict.
- **How to.** The How to button, or `?`, opens a lesson with a tab for each of the three camera controls: what to press, how to sit, the shape to make, the calibration, aiming, firing. Each step is ticked when the tracker itself sees it done (a hand in view, the finger-gun shape, the cross-hairs moved, a shot fired, your head turned and brought back), so the first step without a tick is where things have stopped working. It opens by itself the first time a camera control is switched on, follows whichever control you switch on next, and says so if the camera is blocked. Once closed by hand it only opens when asked.
- **Shotgun hold.** A second way of aiming with the same hand tracker, in place of the finger gun: both hands, as if round a shotgun.
  - *The hold.* The trigger hand has its index finger out along the gun and the other fingers wrapped round the grip, which is the finger-gun shape, so it is found the same way. The other hand is out in front on the fore-end, in any shape. Three readings of that in a row make the hold; after that the hands keep their jobs by where they are and by their left and right labels, whatever shape they take, until both have been out of view for more than half a second.
  - *Aiming.* The gun points the way the fore-end hand lies from the trigger hand. One camera can see only the sideways and up-and-down parts of that, so those are what is used: the offset between the two palms, turned into centimetres by how big the hands look, measured from what it was when you pointed the gun at the middle of the screen, and scaled by your own reach to each side. Being a difference between two hands, it does not care where you sit or how far back you lean. You need not truly point at the screen, and it works better if you do not: pointed straight at the camera, one hand hides the other.
  - *Calibration.* Runs the first time, and again from the Calibrate button under the camera picture; `Esc` skips it. Three steps: point the gun at a dot in the middle, swing it to a dot near the top-left corner, then to one near the bottom-right. Each step waits until the gun has arrived and is holding still, reads it for a second while the dot fills, and pauses on "Got it". It learns how far you swing from the middle to the left, the right, the top and the bottom, each on its own, and keeps them in the browser. Skipped, the reach is about 10 cm sideways and 7 cm up or down to those dots. A reach under 4 cm is not believed. The middle alone is retaken with `C` or the Centre button, and once in each later visit when you first shoulder the gun.
  - *Firing.* Curl the trigger finger. The shot goes where the gun pointed a moment before. Straighten the finger to make ready again; an empty gun reloads when you pull the trigger.
  - *Looking around.* With Head look on, your head turns the view and the gun moves the cross-hairs within it, so the gun follows your head but can stray from it. With Head look off, pushing the cross-hairs against an edge of the screen turns you that way.
  - *The picture in the corner.* A dot on each palm, red for the trigger hand, and a line between them for the gun. The numbers are the trigger finger's reading and the swing in centimetres.
  - *How far it has been tested.* By me, with made-up hands only. How well the tracker keeps hold of two hands that are close together is a guess.
- **Head look.** Your head steers the view and leaves one hand free to be the gun. It shares the camera with the finger gun and works without it too. The picture is read in the browser by MediaPipe's face tracker, which returns the head's attitude as a matrix; no picture leaves the computer.
  - *How it steers.* Like a stick, not like a pair of goggles. Where your face points when you look at the middle of the screen is the centre. A small turn leans the view with you at once (about 0.7° of view for each degree of head). Past a dead patch of 3° the view also keeps turning that way, faster the further you turn, up to about 110° a second at 20° of head; up and down the same, more gently. Bring your head back to the middle and the view stays where it has got to, so the bird you turned towards is still in front of you and you are looking straight at the screen when you shoot.
  - *The centre.* Taken from the first half second after your face is found, and again whenever you press `C` or the Centre head button. A head resting near the centre slowly becomes the new centre, so shifting in your chair does not leave the view creeping.
  - *With the finger gun.* The cross-hairs still follow the fingertip across the screen; the head moves the world behind them. An open second hand no longer turns the view, and a lone hand is the gun or nothing; a second hand held as a fist still walks you. With both trackers running the face is read on every other camera picture, since a head moves slowly beside a hand.
  - *Aim that follows your body.* With head look on, the gun hand is measured from your head, not from the edge of the camera's picture: its offset from the middle of your head, scaled by how far away you are, is put back where it would have been with your head at a reference place. So shifting in your chair or leaning back does not move the aim. The reference is where your head was when you calibrated the finger gun with head look on, which is the way to get the most from this; failing that, where it was when the head was last centred. The middle of the head is taken as 4 cm behind the tracker's own origin, near the axis the head turns on, so that turning your head does not drag the cross-hairs. With head look off the aim works as before.
  - *The picture in the corner.* A yellow diamond on your nose, filled while the view is turning, and a line saying how far your head is from the centre.
  - *The stopwatch.* The last line under the picture, for either tracker: readings a second, how many milliseconds each tracker spends on a picture, and the lag from the camera taking a picture to its reading being ready (smoothing and drawing come after that). The lag is only shown where the browser reports when each picture was taken.
  - *How far it has been tested.* By me: the real face tracker on a still photograph of a face, which settles the conventions and the plumbing, and made-up head angles for the steering. The first real head to try it reported that it worked well. The body-following aim and the left and right labels have only been tried with made-up heads and hands.

## December

In December the barn wears strings of coloured lights: up and over both gables, along both gutters and round the big doors, with a wreath and a few small warm lights on the loft door. The strings hang in swags between nails, the bulbs run red, amber, green, blue, pink in a fixed order, and each shimmers at its own pace. They are splats like everything else (a small bright core and a wide faint halo for each bulb), always built, and hidden by the shader for the other eleven months. `L` or the Advanced panel turns them on out of season.

## The flight model

This section and the next describe the starlings. The other species are variations on it or separate models, described under [Species](#species).

The flock follows StarDisplay (Hildenbrandt, Carere & Hemelrijk 2010, *Behavioral Ecology* 21:1349), which reproduced the shapes and dynamics of real roosting murmurations:

- **Topological neighbours.** Each bird aligns with and is attracted to its 7 nearest neighbours, whatever their distance. Ballerini et al. (2008) measured 6–7 in real flocks. There is a 90° blind cone behind each bird.
- **Separation.** A 0.2 m hard sphere, then a Gaussian falloff. The flock settles at a nearest-neighbour distance of about 1 m.
- **Cohesion weighted by centrality.** Birds on the flock edge are pulled inward more strongly than birds in the interior.
- **Aerodynamics.** Cruise speed is 10 m/s, with relaxation back toward it. Lift scales with v² along the bird's up vector, and gravity acts throughout.
- **Turning only by banking.** Social and roost forces never yaw a bird directly. Their sideways component sets a target roll angle, and the tilted lift vector does the turning. Banked birds lose height in turns, as real ones do.
- **Roost attraction.** Birds turn back toward the roost (the barn and the trees around it) once they stray more than 48 m from it. The preferred altitude drifts slowly, and birds climb over the barn, silo and treetops.
- **Wings.** See [How the animals move](#how-the-animals-move).

## Splitting and rejoining

A flock here is a connected component of the neighbour graph: two groups are separate flocks once no bird in one has a bird of the other among its seven neighbours. Because each bird only attends to seven neighbours, nothing glues a large flock together globally, so it can tear.

**What tears it.**

- *Turning waves.* Every several seconds a bird on the edge of a flock banks into a turn, and the birds that can see it copy the turn about 70 ms later. Attanasi et al. (2014, *Nature Physics* 10:691) tracked real starling turns starting from a few birds and sweeping through the flock at 20–40 m/s. If the wave reaches every bird, the flock wheels as one. If it dies out part-way, the birds that turned fly off as a separate flock. Papadopoulou et al. (2022, *R. Soc. Open Sci.* 9:211898) found the same in pigeons and in their model: one rule produces both collective turns and splits, and sharp turns by birds on the periphery mostly produce splits. Here the wave carries a hop budget: unlimited for a collective turn, short for a split.
- *Falcons.* Birds within 11 m of a falcon flee it, so a stoop through a flock punches a hole and often cuts it in two. Storms et al. (2019) catalogued this in real murmurations, where a flash expansion is often followed by a split.

**What brings it back.**

- *Attraction to flocks in view.* Every bird is drawn toward other flocks within about 100–140 m, with small groups drawn to large ones far more than the reverse. This is the long-range half of the projection model of Pearce et al. (2014, *PNAS* 111:10422), in which birds respond to the silhouette of the whole flock as well as to their neighbours. It is approximated here by attraction to flock centroids, not by an actual rendering of each bird's view.
- *Joining.* A group that comes close to another flock starts matching that flock's heading, and two groups that have just met steer onto their common course. Without this, groups mostly pass through each other and separate again. This rule is my own addition and is not taken from a paper.
- *The roost.* All flocks keep turning back over the same 48 m circle, so their paths cross every few seconds.

A split typically lasts 10–20 seconds, though a small group can stay out for a minute or more. In a 150 s unattended test run with 1,000 birds, the sky held one flock for about half the time, two for most of the rest, and three for a few seconds.

**Escape waves.** When a falcon comes within 24 m, nearby birds perform a "zig": a fast roll to one side and back. Neighbours copy a zig they see after 0.1 s. This follows Hemelrijk, van Zuidam & Hildenbrandt (2015), who showed that the dark bands seen in real murmurations are orientation waves, not density waves. In the sim the wave travels at about 14–16 m/s, close to the 13.4 m/s measured in real flocks.

Other simplifications that are not from the papers: small, slow noise forces keep alignment near the 0.96 measured in real flocks instead of locking at 1.0, and a gentle pull draws birds on a long straggling arm back toward the bulk of their own flock.

## Species

Each species has its own body (built from one parametric splat bird: wingspan, neck, bill, tail, wingtip shape, colours), its own speed and wingbeat, and its own rules. Three of them reuse the starling model with changes suggested by field studies; they are not models fitted to data. Geese and storks are separate models.

| Species | Span | Cruise | Wingbeat | What is different |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Starling | 0.40 m | 10 m/s | 10.4 Hz | The model above. |
| Dunlin | 0.36 m | 14 m/s | 9.6 Hz | Tighter, faster, lower, and over the beach and surf. Turns start every few seconds and pass through the flock with less than half the starlings' copying delay. Brown above, white below, so the flock changes shade as it banks. |
| Jackdaw | 0.70 m | 12 m/s | 5.8 Hz | About 80% of birds have a mate. Mates hold 1.3 m apart and fly each other's course. Each bird attends to 3 neighbours, not 7, and the flock is looser. |
| Pigeon | 0.68 m | 15 m/s | 7.6 Hz | A small kit circling the barn. Every bird has a rank. A bird weights each neighbour's heading by that neighbour's rank, and high-ranked birds wander more and fly slightly faster, so they end up at the front and the rest follow their turns. Three colour morphs. |
| Goose | 1.6 m | 16 m/s | 4.1 Hz | V formation, below. |
| Duck (mallard) | 0.90 m | 19 m/s | 7.2 Hz | The goose model, loosened: small skeins, lopsided lines, a restless lead. Below. |
| White stork | 2.0 m | 10.5 m/s | soaring (2.8 Hz when it flaps) | Thermal soaring, below. |
| *Pteranodon* | 3.8 m and 5.8 m | 12 m/s | soaring (1.6 and 2.2 Hz) | Speculative, below. |

**Ducks.** I found no fitted model of how ducks arrange themselves in flight, so this one is the goose model with three settings changed to match how mallards are described in the field, and the settings are my judgement, not measurements. A skein holds at most 11 birds (29 for geese), so 42 mallards fly as four small parties. One arm may run up to four birds longer than the other before anyone crosses over, so the shapes are mostly lopsided Js and single slanting lines where geese make even Vs. The lead changes every 7 to 21 seconds (25 to 55 for geese). The geometry of a place, the queueing and the phase-locked wingbeats are unchanged. Drakes and hens are coloured differently, 55 drakes to 45 hens, both with the blue wing patch. Cruise speed is an approximate field value; the wingbeat comes from the same formula as every other bird here.

**Dunlin.** The short copying delay stands in for Potts's chorus-line account (1984, *Nature* 309:344), in which a bird times its turn from the wave approaching it and so reacts faster than its own reaction time would allow.

**Jackdaws.** Ling et al. (2019, *Nature Ecology & Evolution* 3:943) tracked wild jackdaws in 3D and found that mated pairs fly together inside the flock and that paired birds interact with fewer neighbours.

**Pigeons.** Nagy et al. (2010, *Nature* 464:890) fitted GPS loggers to homing pigeons and found a hierarchy in who copies whose turns. Here the hierarchy is assigned at random when the kit takes off.

**Geese.** Each goose picks the nearest goose ahead as its guide and flies for a place 1.4 wingspans behind it and 1.06 wingspans to one side, which is where a wingtip leaves rising air (Lissaman & Shollenberger 1970, *Science* 168:1003). The rest is bookkeeping that makes two tidy arms:

- A place belongs to the goose nearest it. A later arrival queues behind that goose, so each arm is a single file.
- A goose keeps to the arm its guide is on. The last goose of the longer arm crosses to the shorter one.
- The front bird steers. After 25 to 55 seconds in front it eases off for a few seconds, the line slides past it and it falls in further back (Voelkl et al. 2015, *PNAS* 112:2115, found ibises share the lead this way).
- Each goose locks its wingbeat to its guide's, delayed by the distance between them, so the flapping runs down the arm as a wave (Portugal et al. 2014, *Nature* 505:399, in ibises).
- More than 29 geese fly as several skeins, each on its own circuit and height.

One part is control engineering and not biology. A goose that matches speed only with the bird ahead amplifies every wobble, and a long arm cracks like a whip in turns. Each goose therefore matches speed partly with its guide and partly with the front bird, using the front bird's course as it was when it passed that point, so the V wheels round together.

**White storks.** Three thermals drift across the field, each lasting about 75 to 120 seconds. A stork circles in one, turning the same way as the rest of the kettle, and climbs at the updraft minus its own sink rate, which grows with bank angle. Near the top it leaves and glides to another thermal, losing height on the way. Bold birds leave first and choose the thermal by its strength and distance; followers leave once much of the kettle has gone and head for the thermal with the most storks (Flack et al. 2018, *Science* 360:911, found leaders and followers of this kind in migrating storks). Specks of thistledown rise in each thermal so the air can be seen. Distances are compressed about tenfold so the cycle fits over the field: real thermals carry storks up a kilometre and the glides run for kilometres, so the glides here are much steeper than a real stork's.

***Pteranodon*.** Nobody has seen a pterosaur fly, so this one is a reconstruction with the guesses marked.

| | Basis |
|---|---|
| Two sizes, two females to each male, large crest on males only | Fossils. Bennett's survey of the Niobrara Chalk specimens found a small class (3.0–4.8 m span) about twice as common as a large class (4.8–6.7 m), differing in crest and pelvis (Bennett 1992, *Journal of Vertebrate Paleontology* 12:422). |
| Thermal soaring: circle up, glide on | Flight modelling. Goto et al. (2022, *PNAS Nexus* 1:pgac023) found *Pteranodon* suited to soaring on updrafts over the sea, as frigatebirds do, though earlier work argued for albatross-style dynamic soaring and the answer depends on uncertain wing drag. |
| Living in groups | Weak, by analogy. Bone beds show some other pterosaurs were gregarious (*Caiuajara*: Manzig et al. 2014, *PLoS ONE* 9:e100005). For *Pteranodon* the skewed sex ratio hints at a colonial, polygynous life like a sea lion's. |
| Leaving a thermal together and gliding in loose files, each behind and beside the one ahead | A guess. Pelicans and frigatebirds do it, and the saving from a neighbour's wake grows with wingspan. |
| Speeds, sink rates, circle radius | Rough figures in the range the flight models give; not fitted. |
| Colours | Invented. Pale below and darker above as in most seabirds, with the male's crest coloured as a display structure. |

The model is the storks' with wider thermals, wider circles and the file-gliding rule added. As with the storks, distances are compressed about tenfold, and there is no sea: a real *Pteranodon* would have been doing this over open water, far from shore.

## How the animals move

The flight paths come from the flocking and soaring models above. This section is about each animal's own body: how its wings beat. The wing has two segments (arm and hand) with a shoulder and a wrist, and the same kinematic model drives every species with different numbers.

- **Wingbeat frequency** is computed, not chosen: Pennycuick's formula (1996, *Journal of Experimental Biology* 199:1613) gives it from body mass, wingspan and wing area, f = m^3/8 g^1/2 b^-23/24 S^-1/3 ρ^-3/8. That gives the values in the table above, and 5.3 Hz for the peregrine. Female *Pteranodon*, being smaller, beat a third faster than males.
- **Stroke timing.** The downstroke takes a set share of each beat (47% in the starling, 55% in the goose), so the wing does not move as a plain sine wave.
- **Upstroke folding.** Birds cut drag on the upstroke by flexing the wrist. How much is measured as the span ratio: wingspan at mid-upstroke over wingspan at mid-downstroke. Small round-winged birds fold a long way in and large birds hardly at all. Here: starling 0.40, pigeon 0.45, jackdaw 0.50, dunlin 0.60, goose 0.75, stork 0.80, *Pteranodon* 0.88. The model solves for the wrist angle that produces each ratio.
- **Wing path and twist.** The arm swings forward on the downstroke and back on the upstroke, the hand wing trails the arm by a fraction of a beat, and it twists leading edge up on the upstroke.
- **Body heave.** The body is pushed up through the downstroke and falls back through the upstroke, so it rides lowest at mid-downstroke. For a wingbeat force that swings by about the bird's weight, the amplitude is g/(2πf)²: 2 mm for a starling, 15 mm for a goose, 10 cm for a flapping *Pteranodon*.
- **Intermittent flight.** Tobalske (1995, *Journal of Experimental Biology* 198:1259) found that starlings pause between bursts of flapping, gliding with wings spread at low speed and bounding with wings folded at high speed. The starlings here do the same: below about cruise speed a pause is a glide, above it a bound, during which the bird follows a short ballistic arc a few centimetres high. Jackdaws flap and glide. Pigeons flap almost continuously with short glides on partly flexed wings and never bound (Tobalske & Dial 1996, *Journal of Experimental Biology* 199:263). Dunlin and geese flap without pause. Storks and *Pteranodon* hold their wings out and flap only when low.
- **Geese** also lock their wingbeat phase to the bird ahead, as described above.

What rests on what: the frequency formula and the flap-glide-bound pattern are published models and measurements. The stroke shares, span ratios, sweep and twist are typical figures from the bird-flight kinematics literature set by hand for each species, not fitted to tracked recordings. For *Pteranodon* every one of them is a guess scaled from large soaring birds, with a body mass of 35 kg for males that is itself disputed.

## The score

The music is generated while it plays by a small synthesizer written in Web Audio inside the page. Nothing is recorded or looped.

- **Harmony.** Chords drift through D major every 13 to 19 seconds: stacked thirds up to the ninth, with the chord on the fifth degree suspended. The next chord is picked at random from the other degrees, with a pull back to the tonic after two chords away. Each of the four pad voices moves to the nearest note of the new chord, so the changes glide.
- **Reacting to the scene.** While a falcon hunts, the harmony turns to D minor within about a second, and turns back when the falcon has gone. Near the sea the pads open up and the bell tones come more often. Larger animals get a slower score: chords last about 40% longer for *Pteranodon* than for starlings.
- **Pads.** Each note is two sawtooth oscillators detuned 15 cents apart plus a triangle an octave up, with a slow pitch drift of its own, through a low-pass filter that opens over about five seconds as the note swells. A quieter sine-and-triangle voice doubles one chord note two octaves up.
- **Bass.** A sine on the chord root with its octave below and a quiet fifth, gliding between roots and swelling into each change.
- **Lead and bells.** A soft triangle lead steps between chord notes within a fifth of its last note, with vibrato that comes in after the note has settled. High sine bells with one inharmonic partial ring on chord notes.
- **Room.** Everything goes through one convolution reverb whose impulse is 5.5 seconds of generated noise that fades and darkens, then a gentle compressor.

The score has been checked for levels and for the key change, but only by measurement in a headless browser, not by ear.

## How the splats work

- **Not everything is a splat.** The shotgun of Splatterhaus mode is a triangle mesh with a depth buffer, drawn last. (The dog is splats: square ones.) Nothing else uses depth: the splats are sorted and blended, and the ground, sea and sky are ray-traced behind them.
- **Data.** Each splat is four RGBA32F texels in one data texture. Static splats (grass, barn, trees, hills, clouds) are uploaded once. Bird splats are rebuilt and re-uploaded every frame into a dynamic region of the same texture.
- **Splat shape.** A splat fades from its centre as exp(−r^2k). With k = 1 that is a true Gaussian, which is right for clouds, haze and far hills. Birds, bark, leaves and grass blades use a larger k (1.3 to 2.4), which keeps the middle of the splat solid and tightens its edge, so things close to the eye look crisp and not out of focus.
- **Birds.** Every bird is posed on the CPU: a body frame from its velocity and bank angle, two-segment wings, and a tail. All the fliers come from one parametric body, drawn at three levels of detail:
  - *Far:* 3 to 9 splats.
  - *Near:* 39 to 63 splats depending on species, with separate top and underside wing splats that fade by facing, so a starling's wings look glossy black from above and grey-brown from below, and a dunlin flashes white when it banks.
  - *Closest:* about 300 splats, for the 56 nearest birds (20 on a phone), the one you are riding and the falcon. Every flight feather is its own splat: eight secondaries straight back from the arm, ten primaries fanning from the wrist with lengths that make the wingtip pointed, rounded or fingered, two rows of coverts, and a seven-feather tail fan. The body has a skin of about 130 small facets in rings from rump to crown, each with its own normal, so the low sun models it. *Pteranodon* has a membrane, not feathers, and keeps its near model.
- **Barn and silo.** About 36,000 splats. The shell is surface splats laid out on panels, each with its thin axis along the surface normal so the sunset lights it properly. Fine detail is drawn on top with long, thin splats used as lines: board-and-batten strips, white corner boards, rakes and bargeboards, window casings and glazing bars, standing seams and patched sheets on the gambrel roof, gutters and downspouts.
  - *Front:* X-braced sliding doors standing ajar on a steel track, with light inside and a lantern above; a hay hood with a hoist beam, pulley and rope; hay spilling from the loft door; a fieldstone foundation; Virginia creeper up one corner.
  - *Sides:* windows, some lit from inside; a painted barn quilt on the sunlit wall; a ladder left leaning; a lean-to with a tin roof, posts and stacked straw bales.
  - *Roof:* a louvred cupola with a weathercock, lightning rods with glass balls, and a few starlings perched on the ridge.
  - *Yard:* milk cans, a barrel, bales, an old wagon wheel, and a water trough in the paddock.
  - *Silo:* concrete staves, steel hoops, a ribbed dome, a caged ladder and a filler pipe.
- **Meadow.** The meadow covers the whole field in three nested rings that tile around the camera. Within 17 m every blade, seed head and flower is its own splat. From 14 to 68 m each splat is a tussock or a patch of flowers, and from 55 to 272 m a larger one. Beyond that, and underneath everything, is a ray-traced ground plane in the same colours.
- **World maps.** Two 512 m textures drive all three rings and the ground plane, so they always agree: one holds dryness, flower density, dominant species and grass vigour; the other holds short grass, shade, bare earth and lamplight. Flower splats carry random numbers instead of a colour. The shader turns each into a poppy, oxeye daisy, cornflower, buttercup, knapweed or clover from the species map at its world position, or hides it where the density map is low. That is what makes the flowers grow in drifts.
- **Track, paddock and shadows.** A two-rut farm track runs from the barn doors through a field gate and across the meadow. The white-fenced paddock beside the barn is grazed short. The long sunset shadows of the barn, silo and every tree are painted into the shade map, so they fall across grass, flowers and ground alike.
- **Fences.** Post-and-rail fences enclose the barn field and run down to where the dunes begin, with a five-bar gate where the track crosses and shrubs along the lines, some with berries.
- **Trees.** Eight kinds, all in summer leaf, each with its own generator: spreading oaks, maples, white-barked birches in clumps, conical spruces with drooping sprays, a row of Lombardy poplars, a weeping willow (a forked bole and arching limbs under a dome, hung with clumped curtains of withies that arch out, taper, hang longest at the edge and sway more toward their tips), a small apple orchard behind the barn, and one dead snag. Leaves are translucent splats that glow when backlit and sway slightly. Each leaf is shaded by where it sits in its clump: darker deep inside, underneath and on the side away from the sun, brightest on the sunward skin, which is what gives a crown its modelling. The copses out on the horizon are small trees of the same kind, a stem and sixteen leaf masses each, so they still read as trees when you fly out over them. Splats right by the eye fade out, and any whose centre is far off screen while its projection is huge are dropped, so nothing smears across the view.
- **Coast.** The shoreline, the beach profile and the dune heights are plain functions of position, written once in JavaScript and once in GLSL, so the coast runs on without repeating. Going seaward the flowers stop, the sward thins into clumps of marram, and the ground rises into two ridges of dunes up to about 4 m with a low gap where the sea shows through from the field.
- **Dunes.** The dune surface is splats: three rings of overlapping sand discs, like the meadow rings, each laid on the dune by the shader and tilted to the slope, so dunes hide what is behind them. Marram blades and tussocks are the meadow's own splats, recoloured, made taller and lifted onto the dune.
- **Beach and sea.** These are not splats. Like the far meadow, they are shaded in the background pass: the pass finds where each view ray meets the beach or the water. The sea has travelling swell and ripples, reflects the sky more strongly at grazing angles, carries the sun's glitter path, breaks into foam lines near the shore and washes up and down the sand, leaving it wet and reflective. Ripples finer than a pixel are dropped with distance to avoid shimmer.
- **Sand.** The lower beach carries the ripples the last tide left, in patches, and a damp line at the top of the tide. Sand the last wave has just left is wet through and mirrors the sun; each wave strands a line of foam as it draws back. The face of a wave about to break lets green light through.
- **On the beach.** Two rows of groyne posts, driftwood, a wrack line of weed, pebbles and shells, and two groups of boulders and cobbles at the water's edge (all splats). Each boulder is a dark core under a skin of facets, so it takes the sun on one side.
- **Footpath and boardwalk.** A footpath leaves the farm track beyond the gate, crosses the field past the willow and the poplars, and becomes a boardwalk of weathered planks with a post-and-rope rail, running through the gap in the dunes down to the beach.
- **Sky.** The background pass draws the gradient, the sun, a high sheet of cirrus lit from below and drifting slowly, the first stars overhead and away from the sun, and a full moon just clear of the horizon directly opposite the sunset, where a full moon is at that hour. The lower clouds are splats: banks of puffs, warm underneath where the sun reaches them and cool on top. A little noise is added so the long gradients do not band.
- **Advanced panel.** The Advanced button under the title, or `G`, opens a panel that changes how every splat is drawn: size, opacity, edge (the exponent k above, as a multiplier: low is a soft blob, high a hard-edged disc), the pixel filter (the minimum size a splat is blurred to on screen, which is what stops far splats shimmering), and density (draw only a share of the splats). It can also colour each splat separately or by distance, draw outlines only, and turn off the depth sort, which shows why back-to-front order matters for see-through splats.
- **Sort.** Each meadow ring is stored in a 12×12 grid of cells, so cells outside the ring or the view are skipped without reading their splats. What survives is ordered with a 16-bit counting sort, linear in depth out to 40 m and logarithmic beyond.
